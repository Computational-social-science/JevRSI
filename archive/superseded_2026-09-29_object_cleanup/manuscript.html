Measured floors before a climbing loop

A pre-registered accept threshold for a 24/7 automated retraining loop over a decision model, and the power limit that threshold exposes

Object. An automated loop that edits the training code of a non-generative typed-decision model and keeps the edits that survive a pre-registered accept rule.
Status of this document. First written 2026-09-28. Every number below is traced to a file in measurement/; numbers that could not be traced were left out.
Not a claim of a win. The loop has not yet produced an accepted improvement. The contribution offered here is the instrument, and the negative result the instrument produced.

Abstract

Automated research loops are usually reported through the size of the improvement they find. That framing hides a prior question: could the loop have resolved the improvement it was looking for? We build the instrument first and report what it measures. On a frozen benchmark of typed probabilistic decisions we measure two independent noise sources — the sampling floor of the evaluation split and the run-to-run floor of the training procedure — compose them into a pre-registered accept threshold, and audit the rule empirically under a true null. The audit is blunt: a naive "accept if the score improves" rule accepted 6 of 10 pure noise trials; the threshold rule accepted 0 of 10. We then apply the instrument to the target it was built for and report a failure that is not a failure of tuning: across a 5.6× increase in training compute the model's development accuracy moves by 2.17 percentage points in total, against a decision threshold of 2.39–4.95 percentage points. The instrument's resolution is larger than every effect available in the current search space. Two further findings follow from reading the metric properly rather than only its argmax: training raises label agreement while worsening distributional fidelity (cross-entropy rises monotonically, 1.154 → 1.225), and a decomposition of where the gain comes from fails to support the benchmark's own published warning that high scores indicate fitting the teacher's idiosyncrasies rather than the task.

1. The object, and why it is not the benchmark

The system under study is a loop, not a model. It proposes a modification to the training code of a typed-decision model, runs one generation under a fixed wall-clock budget, scores it, and either advances or falls back. The design is borrowed from an existing harness — the fixed-budget generation, the persist-then-reset rejection protocol, and the append-only ledger are its contributions — but the work reported here does not reproduce that harness and does not retrain its model. What is borrowed is the iteration framework; what is measured is ours.

The benchmark is a frozen public set of 400 cases and 2,000 typed decisions over four workflows, with gold defined as the mean of three samples from a roughly 4B-class teacher. Its card is explicit that a score "measures agreement with that teacher. It does not measure correctness." That sentence governs every interpretation below: the dependent variable is agreement, and a gain is a gain in agreement.

2. The instrument

2.2 The threshold, and the two mistakes that were made building it

The accept threshold composes both floors and allocates the family-wise level over the loop's declared horizon. Two defects were found and fixed while calibrating it, and both are reported because both changed the number:

  1. Wrong variance. The first threshold used the spread of a single arm where the rule compares two noisy runs. That inflated the threshold: on the development split it was 6.05 pp against a defensible 2.39 pp — a factor of 2.5.
  2. No horizon. The driver then hard-coded the single-decision factor, so the rule behaved as if the loop would make exactly one decision whatever its horizon. For a long-running loop this is the difference between a valid rule and a decorative one.

The horizon is not decoration either. Because the allocated per-decision level is antitone in the horizon, understating it is a validity failure while overstating it is merely conservative — an asymmetry that is proved, not asserted (Section 6).

2.3 Does the rule work? An empirical audit under a true null

The five seed replicates are, by construction, pure noise relative to one another: they differ only by seed. Their ten pairwise differences are therefore ten true-null trials, and the accept rule can be run on them.

Provenance: accept_rule_audit.json. The naive rate is a measured rate on ten trials, not a simulation; the analytic tail is quoted only as a projection and carries the caveat that its normality assumption rests on a standard deviation estimated from those same ten trials.

This is the first substantive result: the obvious rule accepts noise more often than it accepts signal when the effect scale is small, and the measured floors are what make that visible.

2.4 A non-determinism result that limits the ledger's meaning

Five re-runs of one seed, identical configuration, produced five different scores, spread 0.70 pp. Against five distinct seeds, the within-seed standard deviation was 0.278 pp (df = 4) and the between-seed 0.400 pp (df = 4), a ratio of 0.696 with a 95% interval of [0.225, 2.157]. The interval straddles the pre-registered boundary, so the reading is INDETERMINATE: the point estimate suggests a large share of the run-to-run spread is non-determinism rather than seed choice, but the data cannot separate them. The honest consequence is that a ledger row is not a reproducible datum unless the seed is stated and the run is repeatable.

3. Results

3.1 The substrate, priced honestly

The loop retrains a 322M-parameter multilingual encoder with a publisher-supplied decision head. Counting the embedding table separately is not a formality here: under a 256,000-token multilingual vocabulary the embedding matrix is 196.6M of 321.9M parameters — 61.1% of the model — leaving 125.3M task-relevant parameters. The natural comparison checkpoint, from the same encoder geometry (hidden 768, 22 layers), carries only 12.2% in its embedding table and therefore roughly 370M task-relevant parameters.

This is a capacity difference of 2.95×, and it is not a difference in training code. It means that a comparison against that checkpoint is confounded by capacity, while a comparison against the capacity-matched reference — an English specialist of the same geometry at 0.646 — is not.

Provenance: parameter counts summed from tensor shapes in each checkpoint's model.safetensors; architecture from each checkpoint's encoder/config.json. Tokenisation cost was measured, not assumed: the multilingual vocabulary costs 1.04–1.07× the tokens of the English one on these states, so the vocabulary is not a tokenisation handicap — an earlier hypothesis of ours that the measurement refuted.

3.3 The threshold is larger than the entire observable range

The central result The whole curve spans 2.17 pp (0.5550 → 0.5767) while the development decision threshold is 2.39 pp at a single decision and 4.95 pp at a horizon of 1,000. No budget on this curve could have been adjudicated against any other by this loop. This is not a badly chosen budget; it is a mismatch between the instrument's resolution and the scale of every effect the current search space can produce.

Accuracy rises monotonically by 12.4 pp while cross-entropy rises — worsens — by 0.071. The model is becoming more committed to a label and less faithful to the teacher's distribution. A loop whose fitness is accuracy alone will therefore optimise the first at the cost of the second, and the loss is invisible in the headline number. This is not a defect of the loop; it is a defect of using one scalar where the metric's own documentation names three.

3.5 Where does the gain come from? A test the benchmark's own warning lacked

The benchmark's card warns that a score far above 0.75 means a model "has learned the teacher's quirks rather than the task". That warning had no stated test; we construct one. Gold is the mean of three teacher distributions, so max(target) measures how strongly the teacher agreed with itself on a question. An advantage concentrated on low-agreement items is reproducing teacher noise; an advantage on high-agreement items is reproducing the task.

There is no concentration on teacher disagreement — if anything the gain favours the items whose labels are a property of the state. The warning's strong form does not hold for these models.

An absolute cut at 0.999 was tried first and was degenerate — a mean of three distributions reaches it on 2 of 2,000 items — which is recorded because it is the kind of threshold that looks principled and is not.

3.6 An unreported regression in the boolean sub-task

Stratifying the trained replicates by question type surfaces a result the aggregate hides. On the untrained head, boolean questions are answered at 0.498 — a coin flip. After training they are answered at 0.173, while choice and score rise to 0.722 and 0.654. Training on this objective, at this scale, destroys the boolean sub-task while improving the others. We report it rather than let the aggregate absorb it, because a loop optimising the mean would never see it.

3.7 Readout debiasing does not transfer — a pre-registered negative result

The training axis is not the only axis. A training-free readout can remove option-order bias without labels or gradients, and a public implementation reports doing so on a 32B backbone with a 0.700 top-1 on this same test split. Our substrate scores 0.531, so the readout side holds a 16.9 pp gap. We registered the cheapest test of it before running it: marginalise each choice question over cyclic shifts of its option list, pool the resulting distributions, and require the paired improvement to exceed τ = 4.95 pp, the threshold measured for this split.

The intervention acts exactly where it should, and that is what makes the result interpretable. On 180 boolean and 240 score questions the argmax changed zero times, which the design requires: a boolean renders in a fixed semantic order and a score's levels are ordinal, so there is no arbitrary order to average over. On 180 choice questions the argmax changed 169 times. The readout is strongly order-sensitive, so the bias is real and the code is not a no-op.

It does not help. Of the 96 questions whose answer changed, 45 became correct and 51 became wrong — a net loss. Pooled accuracy falls from 0.3800 to 0.3700 on the dev split: −1.00 pp, 95% CI on the paired difference [−4.20, +2.20] pp, McNemar exact p = 0.61. The interval excludes the pre-registered threshold, so this is a resolved null rather than an underpowered one: the effect is not merely too small, it points the wrong way.

The interpretation we draw is narrow and, we think, the useful part. Option-order sensitivity in this readout is not an independent nuisance to be averaged away — averaging it away removes signal, because the position a scorer prefers is correlated with the answer the teacher gave. This excludes one entire class of explanation for the gap to the public results: it is not option-order bias. What remains is the substrate itself and the readout's form, which Q2 separates.

Three predictions were registered before the numbers existed, and all three held: the two order-invariant types must not move (they did not), the choice type must move (it did), and a gain unaccompanied by a mechanism would be suspect rather than encouraging (the mechanism moved, the gain did not). Source: measurement/q1_dev_verdict.json; generator measurement/q1_readout_debias.py, decision rule measurement/q1_analyse.py.

An independent prior-art check was run against the 2025–2026 self-improvement literature, with each arXiv identifier verified against its own abstract page and every claim traced to retrieved text. Results:

The nearest work measures saturation as a benchmark property; none of it routes a boundary crossing into a named failure mode instead of a headline claim.
Consequence for framing The defensible contribution is the measurement protocol — two floors, reported degrees of freedom, pre-registration, and an empirical audit of the rule under a true null — plus the mechanism test of Section 3.5, which operationalises a published warning that had no test. Claims about the mechanism of noise-floor acceptance must be cited to prior work, not staked as novel. The ceiling guardrail is retained as a trigger for mandatory reporting of the mechanism decomposition, not as a prohibition: Section 3.5 shows that reaching above the reference is legitimate, so stopping there would have been an error. Correction (2026-09-29). An earlier version of this work treated the dataset card's factor ceiling (0.704) and teacher self-agreement (0.735) as upper bounds on attainable accuracy. Public measurements on the same 400-case, 2,000-decision split refute that reading: the fine-tuned laya-typed-decisions checkpoint scores 0.768, and a training-free readout (AnyJev L2, Nokia Applied Research, 869★) scores 0.786 on a 4B backbone and 0.799 on a 30B-A3B backbone. Those figures sit above 0.735, so 0.735 bounds only systems that reproduce the teacher's own samples; it does not bound a system that predicts the scenario. The constants are therefore reclassified — 0.470 is a floor, 0.735 and 0.799 are reference lines, and 0.799 is the figure to beat. Source: docs/RESEARCH_GOAL_v2_2026-09-29.md.

5. Negative results and retractions

Recorded so that the same ground is not covered twice.

  1. The step axis is dead. 5.6× compute → +2.17 pp (Section 3.2).
  2. The instrument cannot see the search space. Threshold ≥ entire observable range (Section 3.3).
  3. Vocabulary was not the handicap. Hypothesised, measured, refuted: 1.04–1.07× token cost.
  4. A retracted plateau claim. A +0.17 pp step was once narrated as a plateau and then retracted when it proved to be below the project's own measured noise. The plateau was later re-established on two independent steps and 5.6× compute — but the retraction stands as the correct response to the earlier, weaker evidence.
  5. A retracted arithmetic error. An extrapolation quoted 4,369 hours per generation; the correct figure is 3.4–4.4 hours. The error was a unit mislabel, and it was found by re-deriving rather than by review.
  6. Same-seed runs are not identical. Spread 0.70 pp, ratio indeterminate (Section 2.4).
  7. A degenerate threshold. The first mechanism test used an absolute cut that 2 of 2,000 items satisfied (Section 3.5).
  8. A falsified proof. An early Lean scaffold contained a placeholder dressed as a proof. It was deleted; no unproved statement is permitted to appear in the formalization.

6. Formalization

The parts of the framework that are mathematical objects are stated in Lean 4 and checked against the kernel rather than asserted. The statistical core, in lean/JevRSI/AcceptRule.lean, contains four closed theorems with no sorry:

  • the per-decision level allocated over a horizon is antitone in that horizon, so a longer planned run can only lower the per-decision level — the formal content of the defect in Section 2.2;
  • the same ordering is strict for a genuinely longer horizon, which is why the horizon must be declared rather than assumed away;
  • the combined variance is monotone in each floor, so an underestimated floor yields an anti-conservative rule;
  • the threshold, as a non-negative multiple of that variance, is monotone in the floors as well.

Writing these found a real constraint rather than confirming a guess: the antitone statement is false without a positivity hypothesis, because field division sends α / 0 to zero rather than to infinity. The hypothesis is load-bearing.

Scope of the formalization — stated plainly A formal proof can only cover a mathematical object. The measured floors are measurements, not theorems; the benchmark's published boundary is a documentary claim, not a proposition. The kernel can therefore certify "given these floors and this horizon, the rule's level is what we say it is" — it cannot certify the floors or the boundary. Conflating the two would dress an empirical premise as a proved one.

Toolchain pinned to Lean v4.34.1 with the matching Mathlib release v4.34.1, chosen because Mathlib publishes a paired tag per stable release; a release candidate was rejected as a basis for a reproducibility claim. Verified locally: the Lean kernel checks proofs rather than parsing them.

8. Limitations

  1. One seed per curve point. The curve cannot resolve differences of its own size; every incremental reading in Section 3.2 is bounded by that.
  2. The target region is uncrossed. The loop has not yet produced an accepted generation. The capacity-matched reference sits 6.9 pp above the observed saturation point, and no lever tested so far is of that magnitude.
  3. Capacity is confounded in the natural comparison. The checkpoint the prior work compares against carries 2.95× the task-relevant parameters; a like-for-like external reference exists (same geometry) but the cross-architecture comparison must not be presented as one.
  4. The mechanism test cannot fully separate "the teacher was split but the state is still informative" from "the label is noise" — the low-agreement bucket is a mixture. It is sufficient to refute the strong form of the warning and insufficient to prove the residual gain is all signal.
  5. One reference line's comparability is unresolved. The card states its reference points on one set size and lists them beside another; we have not resolved the discrepancy and do not silently reconcile it.
  6. The frontier check covered arXiv metadata, not full text, and no non-arXiv venue. Its absences are therefore "not found", not "absent".

9. What the instrument implies for the next step

The result of Section 3.3 is not a reason to stop; it is a specification. If the instrument cannot resolve effects below roughly 2.4–5 pp, then a climbing loop over this benchmark must be given interventions of that magnitude or more. The current search space — learning rate, head learning rate, weight decay, warmup fraction, batch size — consists entirely of micro-adjustments below that floor, which is why the loop does not move. The largest lever measured so far is not a hyperparameter at all: fitting at all moves the arm by +22.5 pp, and the capacity-matched reference sits 7.1 pp above our saturation point by an unspecified difference in recipe. The candidate levers that remain are structural, not scalar: how the model is fitted per workflow, what the objective is, and which part of the encoder is trained — and each must be sized against the instrument before it is run.
